# Four Numbers That Do the Heavy Lifting: How the Screener's Momentum, Value, Quality and Risk Scores Work

**Summary:** The screener condenses dozens of raw metrics into four scores — Momentum, Value, Quality and Risk. Each blends several underlying measures, so no single flattering (or misleading) ratio can dominate. Used together they let you build very different screens from the same table: cheap recovery plays, expensive-but-excellent compounders, or steady income payers. This piece explains what each score measures, why composites beat single ratios, which settings suit which style, the traps to avoid, and what to check before you buy anything the screen surfaces.

## Why screen at all?

The London market has well over a thousand listed companies. Nobody can follow them all, and the ones you hear about — in the press, on bulletin boards — are usually the ones everyone else already owns. A screener inverts that: you define the characteristics you want and let the whole market compete for your attention. It doesn't tell you what to buy. It tells you where to look, which is most of the battle.

## The four scores

**Momentum (1–10)** measures how a share price has performed over the past year — but deliberately ignores the most recent three months, because very short-term winners tend to snap back. This "12-minus-1" construction is the version academic studies find most persistent. Every stock is then ranked against the rest of the universe, so a 9 means "top fifth of the market", not an absolute return. Momentum works because good news travels slowly: earnings upgrades and re-ratings arrive in sequences, not single jumps.

**Value (0–10)** asks how cheap a stock looks through up to six different lenses: forward price/earnings, price-to-book, price-to-sales, free-cash-flow yield, dividend yield, and a growth-adjusted measure (PEGY). Each available lens is scored and averaged, so a loss-maker with no P/E isn't unfairly punished — and, more importantly, a stock can't look cheap just because one distorted ratio says so. The forward P/E uses analyst estimates rather than statutory earnings, so a one-off write-down can't manufacture phantom cheapness.

**Quality (0–10)** rewards businesses that earn high returns *and* have done so consistently. It scores return on capital, return on equity, and gross, operating and cash margins — awarding points both for clearing an absolute bar (e.g. ROIC above 10%) and for being at or above the company's own multi-year median. That second test matters: plenty of companies post one great year at the top of a cycle. Quality is trying to find the ones for whom great years are normal.

**Risk (1–10, higher = riskier)** estimates the chance of serious permanent loss. It blends balance-sheet strength (Altman Z-type distress measures), leverage and interest cover, and share-price volatility. Crucially, it's sector-aware: banks, insurers, investment trusts and asset-heavy businesses like utilities are scored with models suited to their balance sheets, because a bank measured like a manufacturer always looks bankrupt.

## Why composites beat single measures

Screen on one ratio and you get whatever that ratio's blind spot produces. The lowest-P/E list in the UK is reliably full of value traps: shrinking businesses, cyclicals at peak earnings, and firms with problems the market has already spotted. A composite is harder to fool — a stock has to look cheap on *several* independent bases to earn a high Value score, and pairing it with Quality and Risk filters out the cheap-for-a-reason names. The scores are designed to disagree with each other; the interesting stocks are the ones where the disagreement resolves in your favour.

## Complementary measures

The scores compress a lot, so a few standalone metrics are worth adding as columns. The **Piotroski F-Score** (0–9) counts year-on-year fundamental improvements — a good "is this business getting better or worse?" check on anything Value flags. **Altman Z** is the raw distress number behind the Risk score, useful when you want to see the actual margin of safety. **Revenue growth** separates cheap-and-shrinking from cheap-and-growing. **Dividend yield with FCF cover** tells you whether an income stock can actually afford its payout. And **market cap** is a filter in its own right: below the small-cap line, spreads widen and liquidity risk becomes real.

## Settings for different stock types

- **Quality compounders:** Quality ≥ 7, Risk ≤ 4, ignore Value. You'll pay up; the bet is that consistency persists longer than the market prices in.
- **Recovery / deep value:** Value ≥ 7, Piotroski ≥ 6, Risk ≤ 6. The F-Score is doing the real work — it separates recovering businesses from melting ice cubes.
- **Momentum with a safety net:** Momentum ≥ 8, Risk ≤ 5. Pure momentum lists fill up with speculative small caps; the risk cap removes most of them.
- **GARP (growth at a reasonable price):** Quality ≥ 6, Value ≥ 5, Momentum ≥ 6 — the "nothing terrible anywhere" screen. Shorter lists, fewer surprises.
- **Income:** dividend yield ≥ 4%, Quality ≥ 5, Risk ≤ 5. The quality and risk floors are what keep you out of yield traps.

## Pitfalls to watch

**Sector blindness.** Banks and insurers screen "cheap" on P/E and price-to-book almost by definition. Compare financials with financials, and read Value alongside the sector column, not alone.

**Missing data.** A blank score isn't a bad score — small caps and recent listings often lack the history to compute momentum or medians. Don't let sorting quietly bury them.

**Everything is backward-looking.** The scores describe the company that filed the last accounts, not the one that just issued a profit warning. A screen run the morning after results can be a year out of date in spirit.

**Over-filtering.** Stack six strict filters and you'll get three stocks, all flattered by accounting quirks. Looser filters plus your own judgement beats a screen tightened until only anomalies survive.

## After the screen: the next layer of checks

A screen produces candidates, not decisions. For each shortlisted stock: read the last two or three RNS statements (especially the outlook paragraphs), check the news flow for anything the accounts haven't caught up with, look at the fair-value comparison against sector peers, glance at analyst coverage and how estimates have moved, and check short interest — a heavily shorted "bargain" deserves extra scepticism. Then read the actual report and accounts of anything that survives. The screener's job is to make sure the two hours you spend reading are spent on the right company.

## Other ways in

Screening is one funnel among several. Regulatory news itself is another — unexpected earnings upgrades and contract wins often precede the fundamentals showing up in any screen (that's what the High Impact RNS page is for). Trending and most-viewed lists show where attention is flowing; the sector heatmap shows where money is. The best ideas often come from crossing two funnels: a stock that pops up on a news-driven list *and* already screens well is telling you the same story twice.
